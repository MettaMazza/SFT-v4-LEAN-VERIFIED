CAN THE RATIO STAY THE SAME?
A short substitution puzzle
Maria Smith · Ernos Labs

START HERE
Begin with the word AB. At each step replace every A with AB and every B with A, simultaneously. For example:

AB → ABA → ABAAB

Let a count the A's and b count the B's. Initially (a,b) = (1,1).

QUESTIONS
1. Find the next five count pairs without writing out the longer words.
2. Explain why the two counts never share a common factor greater than 1.
3. Could the ratio a/b ever remain exactly unchanged after a substitution step?
4. More generally, could any positive rational ratio remain unchanged under this count rule?

Try the questions before reading the solution below.

HINTS
Each A contributes one A and one B to the next word.
Each B contributes one A.
For the last question, write the ratio as p/q in lowest terms.


WORKED SOLUTION

1. Count the contributions.
The next pair is (a+b,a). Starting from (1,1), the next five pairs are:
(2,1), (3,2), (5,3), (8,5), (13,8).

2. Preserve common divisors.
A number divides both a+b and a exactly when it divides both a and b: subtract a from a+b in one direction, and add a to b in the other. Therefore gcd(a+b,a) = gcd(a,b). Since the initial gcd is 1, it stays 1 at every step.

3–4. Rule out an unchanged positive rational ratio.
The next ratio is (a+b)/a = 1 + 1/(a/b).
An unchanged ratio r would therefore satisfy r = 1 + 1/r.

Write r = p/q with positive coprime integers p and q. Multiplying by pq gives:
p² = pq + q².

The right-hand side is divisible by q, so q divides p². If q were greater than 1, it would have a prime divisor l. That prime would divide p², hence p, contradicting the coprimality of p and q. Thus q = 1.

The same equation shows that p divides q². With q = 1, positivity forces p = 1.

But substituting p = q = 1 gives 1 = 2. This contradiction excludes every positive rational fixed ratio, including each positive rational ratio produced by the substitution.

DISCUSSION
Which parts used the particular starting word, and which applied to any positive rational ratio? The first six count pairs illustrate the recurrence; the last argument covers all positive rational candidates.
